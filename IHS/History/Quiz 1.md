## EH Carr:
### 1. The Historian and His Facts
#### Acton, Oct. 1986:
- Ultimate/Definite history is possible given enough human effort
- Historians should bring together all the documents to form universally valid conclusions
- High optimism on the existence of a single account/perspective on history

#### George Clarke, 60 years + Acton,
- Historians expect work to be superseded again and again.
- Universal account of history does not exist.
- Any historical judgement involves people and perspectives, and therefore cannot be objective

Acton - Positive belief and confidence of the Victorian age
George Clarke - Post-war realism and scepticism

#### Mr. Gradgrind, Ranke's Positivist view and the Importance of Facts:
- Job of historian is to show how the past really was
- History as a science based on the cult of facts
- First find the facts, then draw your conclusions from them, i.e, the empirical nature of knowledge.
- Facts are independent of the observer's consciousness and perspective, i.e, the common-sense view of history.
- One must not be able to ascertain the nature and feelings of the writer on looking at his work. Must be so objective

#### The Historical Fact:
- Basic facts: Raw, verifiable statements about the past events that are same for all historians.
	- The Battle of Hastings happened in <b>1066 in Hastings</b> 
	- Caesar <b>crossed the Rubicon</b>
- Basic facts are not always relevant to the historian, and collecting them is not the job of a historian. They are the raw materials available to the historian, rather than their product.
- A historical fact is a fact that is used by a historian to support a certain narrative. The historian chooses which facts are important
	- The Battle of Hastings had <b>major consequences to the history of Britain</b>.
	- The thousands of normal people who crossed the Rubicon are <b> irrelevant </b>
- A historian is naturally selective because of the nature of their work.
- A fact can be made historical only upon its successful interpretation as one by multiple other historians - <b> History is a series of accepted judgements - Prof. Barraclough </b> 
	- The recorded history of Greece in 5th Century BCE, is mostly the Athenian perspective. We know little about the perpectives of the Spartans, of their slaves, etc
	- The picture of medieval man as devout is drawn by the chroniclers of the time, mostly not by the common man himself, and was subsequently destroyed by the revolutions of the Spring of Nations

#### The Evolution of Facts:
- Early and ancient historians did follow the above mentioned selective process for writing history, deeming only <b>certain facts as relevant</b>.
	- Historians need ignorance - the ignorance to selectively choose what and what not to deem significant.
	- The best historians are ignorant about their selected period of history.
- The 19th century's Positivist view was in contrast to this opinion.
	- History is a collection of universally accepted basic facts - regardless of their consequences and significance.
	- Historians collected as many facts as they could, irrespective of their value
	- <b>Accumulation of facts is the foundation of history</b>
- The positivist's attraction to facts also turned into an attraction to documents.
- But <b>documents are written via the selective process</b>. The author deems what is necessary and what is irrelevant while writing. They cannot go beyond the author's message. The <b>historian must still process</b> the writing and its facts
	- Gustav Streseman, Foreign Minister of the Weimar Republic, left behind lots of documents relating to his thoughts/beliefs about the politics of the world upon his death in 1929
	- His policies regarding the western economies were deemed as brilliant successes - The League of Nations, Withdrawal of the Allies from the Rhineland, etc
	- But his policies regarding the eastern economies, i.e, Soviet Union, were deemed irrelevant and bore no significant consequences and <b>were under-represented</b> in the subsequent publications of his work
	- Also, these documents <b>only tell Streseman's perspective of the world</b>, of the discussion that he had and what he felt happened, i.e, the selective process.

#### Positivism and the State of the World
- The 19th century was filled with confidence and optimism.
	- Facts alone were deemed sufficient and cross-analysis was weak
	- Ranke - <b>Divine will would give the meaning of history</b>, given that we collect enough facts
	- Burckhardt - <b>Humans cannot know the divine plan</b>
- British historians - Meaning of history is <b>implicit and self-evident</b>
- History can be studied in a scientific and structured manner
- Positivism - Related to the policy of <b>laissez-faire</b> (free market) and a general self-confident outlook on the world

#### Post Positivism - Modern Methods and the Collingwood View of History
- Croce - All history is contemporary history, i.e, **seeing** the past through the eyes of the present and its problems
- Historians should not record - they should <b>evaluate</b>
- A historical fact is created by historians
- This type of thinking was realised due to post-war (WW1) realism.
- Collingwood - The phliosophy of history is concerned with both the past and the historian's thoughts on it
	- History - Inquiries of the historian and the series of past events he inquires upon
	- **A past act is dead until the historian inquires upon it**
	- All history is the history of thought and the history of reasoning behind human actions
	- It is the experience within a historian's mind
	- History is dependent on empirical evidence but **is itself not empirical** - it is built on subjectivity
- No fact is pure, and they always **pass through the judgement of the recorder**
	- G.M. Trevelyan's England under Queen Anne is written in this style. It is a reconstruction of that period of history in Trevelyan's mind.
- The historian must re-enact in his mind the event under inquiry, and the reader must in turn, re-enact the historian's thought process. 
- **Study the historian before his facts**

#### Historian's need for Imaginative Understanding
- Imaginative Understanding - Similar to **Sympathy** but not exactly
- 19th century historians were **repelled by the Medieval superstitions**
	- Grew up with the beliefs of **nationalism over religion** (Spring of Nations)
	- Could not understand the actions of the Medieval man.
		- Burckhardt - Scandalous that a creed to place its salvation over the integrity of the nation
		- Believed it is right to kill in the name of one's country, but not in the name of one's religion.
- Modern history also shares similar concerns
	- Western records of the Soviet Union 
- **Inability to understand the other party -> Other party appears malign and senseless**
- **History cannot be written unless we look into the mind of the people we are writing about**

#### Contemporary History
- We can view the past only through the eyes of the present and in the light of its problems and beliefs
- Historian is **bound to the present** - his words are defined in the ways of the present
- Any deep changes in the belief system will result in significant changes to how history is perceived
	- The change is the power dynamics between Catholicism and Protestantism affected the attitude of historians towards figures like Loyola, Luther and Cromwell (Prominent Catholic, Protestant and Puritan respectively)
- No historian is immune to this effect and can be clearly seen just by the superficial analysis of some sources.

#### Drawbacks of the Collingwood View of History
- Collingwood, in his remarks against the cut-and-paste nature of history, almost treats history as entirely a product of the human brain. 
- There are infinite possible interpretations, **none more correct than the other.**
	- But just because interpretation is a crucial part of studying history, it does not mean than history is absent of any objective ideals.
- If a historian looks at his event of inquiry only through the eyes of the present, he will fall into a purely practical view of the facts, i.e, history will be viewed as a tool to solve the present, not as a way to seek the truth. (ex: writing propaganda)
	- **The validity of knowledge is the validity of this purpose**
	- This drawback tends to create nostalgia for the Positivistic approach
- The duty of the historian is not only to see whether his facts are accurate.
	- He must bring into picture all possible facts relevant to his event under inquiry.
	- He cannot eliminate interpretation.
	- The processes of "input" (sources, facts, etc) and "output" (writing, recording) happen simultaneously and are considered a single process.
- **History should be balanced between its view as a collection of facts, and its view as the subjective product of the historian's mind.**
- **The dichotomy between fact and interpretation** will be encountered again and again.
	- Man is not involved in his environment and is not unconditionally subjected to it, but he is also not independent of it and its unconditional master.
	- Similar is the relation between a historian and his theme of inquiry. He is neither the humble slave (positivism) nor the master (Collingwood)

#### Conclusion
- The historian starts with a provisional selection of facts and a provisional interpretation upon which the selection has be done, and as he works, the set of facts and the interpretation undergo subtle changes through the reciprocal action of one or the other
- The reciprocal action also involves reciprocity between present and the past, since the historian lives in and is constrained to the present and his event in inquiry is in the past.
- **The historian without his facts is useless, and the fact without a historian is meaningless and dead.**
- Therefore, history is the continuous process of interaction between a historian and his facts, and a unending dialogue between the past and present.

### 2. Society and the Individual
#### Society or the Individual
- Society and the individual are inseparable, like the hen and the egg. Neither comes first as they are necessary to and complementary to each other.
- Donne - "No man is an island, every man is a piece of the continent, a part of main"
- J. S. Mill, an individualist - "Men are not, when brought together, converted into another kind of substance"
	- The fallacy - The assumption that **they were anything before being brought together**.
- Every human is **subject to the forces of society** once he is born
	- His way of thought and language is a **social acquisition** - defined by the society around him.
	- The individual apart from society would be mindless and speechless.
	- **Society shapes of way of thinking and our behaviour**
	- Any of his act (except suicide) **involves his participation in society** in one way or another

#### Growth of Society
- It is commonly said that primitive man is less individual and more moulded by society than modern man
	- This is due to the increased uniformity, i.e, absence of diversity in the opportunities and occupations available.
	- Modern societies, due to their increased diversity, thus increase the individualisation of people.
- However, **the development of society and the development of individuals go hand in hand**
	- Complex society - the interdependence of different people and different roles and opportunities is complex
	- More developed a society is, **more is the need for the creation of increasingly specialised roles**
- The power of modern society to shape its participants **is no less** than that of a primitive society
- The definition of human nature is dependent on the society that enquires the definition
- Any difference between people from different societies can be linked to the differences between the two societies themselves
- **Civilised man is moulded by society as much as society is moulded by them.**

#### The Cult of Individualism
- The most pervasive of modern historical myths.
- Began with the Renaissance, where man became a **"spiritual individual"** and recognised himself as such, from being conscious of himself only as a part of a group, race, family or corporation.
- Was later connected with the rise of Capitalism and Protestantism, with the beginning of the Industrial Revolution and the doctrine of laissez-faire.
- The **rights of man and the citizen** as defined in the French Revolution, were the **rights of the individual**.
- Individualism was deeply rooted in the philosophies of the 19th century, like utilitarianism. 
- However there is no clear separation between the development of the individual and that of society.
	- **The individual did not revolt again society, they merely shaped it.**
	- Social revolutions did not destroy society - they brought new societal groups to positions of power.
	- **The society grew itself through the actions and operations of the individual.**
	- The role and importance of the individual initiative is given by society itself and emphasised its involvement in the societal order
	- The whole process is a social one, representing a certain stage in the development of humanity.
- The ideology created by this period of history is still predominant, especially in western societies, even though many have seen **signs of its end**, due to **the rise of mass democracy** and the gradual **replacement of predominantly individual driven forms of economic production.**
- Any conflict that happens on the grounds of abstract terms like equality and liberty, are struggles not between the individual and the society, rather between different societal groups that are in favour to it and against it.
- **Individualism is a great social movement not the false opposition between individual and society.**
- The common-sense view of history (Positivism) treats it as something written **by individuals about individuals**
	- But the men that the historian studies do not act only on individual needs separated from society, rather they are **a product of society back then.**
	- The historian also bases his work on knowledge that is gathered by the work of multiple people, possibly extending for multiple generations

#### The Historian
- The historian is also an individual human being. He is also a social phenomenon, no different from any other.
- It is as a social product that he approaches the historical facts.
	- The historian is **a part of the very human society that he tries to study**. He is not an external unbiased observer
	- **The historian is part of history**
- The state of the society he lives in determines the perspective with which he sees the past.
	- Grote's History of Greece:
		- Grote: Radical banker in the 1840s
		- Embodies the aspirations of the rising and progressive British middle class, in his picture of the Athenian democracy, where Athens acquired an empire in a fit of absence of mind
		- Neglected the problem of slavery within Athenian society, reflected in **the failure of this British middle class to face the problem of the rising English factory working class.**
	- Mommsen's History of Rome:
		- Mommsen: German liberal, disillusioned by the German revolution of 1848-9, and wrote in the era of Realpolitik (defined by Otto von Bismark as a system of politics defined on practical rather than moral principles)
		- Well known for his idealisation of Caesar, reflected by the **German society's failure to realise its political aspirations.**
	- Looking into a historian's works can also give us an insight into the society that the historian lives in
	- **Great history is written precisely when the historian's vision of the past is illuminated by insights of the problems of the present.**

#### Sir Lewis Namier
- Namier was a true conservative (traditional, averse to change)
- In the pre-war (19th century - 1914) period, historical change was always conceived as something for the better.
- Post WW1 (1920s), change was beginning to be thought of as something for the worse, a rebirth of conservative thinking.
- Namier had no roots in the pre-war period of liberalism and suffered no nostalgia for it, which further fuelled his conservatism.
- Post War reaction could come only in 2 forms - Socialism and Conservatism. Namier chose Conservatism and appeared as the conservative historian.
	- In English history, he chose the last period in which the ruling class had significant power and could alter societal laws and behaviour. An age safe from the dangers of liberalism
	- Namier also chose the failures European Revolutions of 1848, expressing the inefficiency of ideals in face of arms and when confronted by soldiers, claiming that including ideals in politics is futile (<i>Realpolitik</i>)
- Thus, the work of a historian is wholly dependent on the view from which he has approached it and depends on his thought process, moulded by the society of the time.

#### Meinecke
- Grote, Mommsen and Namier were moulded by a single type of political thought.
- But the mind of a historian is also subject to rapid changes within society. Meinecke's works express this
	- His first work in 1907 - Confidence is the nationalistic ideals of the 19th century, of Bismarck's Germany.
	- His second work in 1925 - Bewilderment of the Weimar Republic and the world of politics as a series of unresolved conflicts.
	- His third work in 1936 - A more critical view of Germany, following the rise of Nazism
	- His fourth work in 1946 - History is at the mercy of blind chance, following the defeat of Germany

#### The Historian and his Society
- **There is no more significant pointer to the character of a society than the kind of history it writes or fails to write**
- The historian himself is in flux, his ideals and thoughts subject to the nature of his society and any changes in it
- The work of a historian mirrors the history that he lives in.
- **Before you study the history, study the historian and his historical and social environment**

#### Society and the Historical Fact
- The Bad King John and Good Queen Bess theory of history - the view than what matters in history is the character and behaviour of individuals.
- It is the desire to model **individual genius as the driving force behind history.**
	- Ancient Greeks attributed their achievements to prominent figures in that domain - a form of heroism (epics - Homer, laws and institutions - Lycurgus and Solon)
	- The same inclination again occurs in the Renaissance
- The view that "history is the biography of great men" is still prominent. **Old traditions die hard.**
	- "mass murder of historical characters" - treating them as "puppets of social and economic forces"
- Miss. Wedgwood - **"The behaviour of individuals is more interesting than the their behaviour as a group or class. History can be well written in this bias as well as another; it is neither more or less misleading"**
	- The behaviour of men as individuals is distinct from their behaviour as members of groups or classes and a historian can choose to study either
	- The behaviour of men as individuals is of **the study of the motives behind their actions**
- This view studies events in history as majorly due to the actions of individuals,
	- Karl Marx as the brain-child of Communism
	- The effects of Genghis Khan and Hitler
	- Queen Elizabeth and the Tudor monarchy
	- The Bolshevik Revolution and the stupidity of Nicholas II.
- The individual is by definition a member of a society, i.e, we cannot draw a clear distinction between their behaviours and intents.
	- Psychologism - The view that all social problems **can be reduced into analysis of the individual**
	- The psychologist who does not take into consideration the social environment will fail eventually.
	- Acton - "Nothing causes more error and unfairness in man's view of history, than the interest which is inspired by individual characters", reflects Acton's warning against focusing on the individual.
	- G. M. Young - "Servants talk about the people, gentlefolk discuss things", reflects the elitist view that society is more important than the individual, the other extreme of this view.
- **History should take a balance between the individual and the group, and over emphasis on any one side distorts history.**

#### The Enquiry Behind Motive
- The need to enquire the motive of an action seems weird at first glance.
	- Humans do not always act on conscious motives.
	- But attempts on describing the cause of these unconscious motives may lead to the conclusion of a divine power affecting our actions
- Marx - **History does nothing, it possesses no immense wealth, fights no battles. It is rather man, real living man who does everything, who possesses and fights**
- Carlyle - **Hunger and nakedness and nightmare oppression lying heavily on twenty-five million hearts: this, not the wounded vanities or contradicted philosophies of philosophical advocates, right shopkeepers, rural nobles,  was the prime mover in the French Revolution; as the like will be in all such revolutions, in all countries**
- Lenin - **Politics begin where the masses are; not where there are thousands, but where there are millions, that is where serious politics begin**
- The nameless millions mentioned above are all individuals, not mindless. No historian will ignore the common concern of millions. Numbers count in history
- Actions of individuals can also have consequences that were not desired or intended by the doers.
- Mandeville - **Private vices - Public benefits**
- Individuals can work to satisfy some grand purpose, even though they believe themselves to fulfilling only their own desires.
- Marx - **Human beings enter into definite and necessary relations which are independent of their will**
- Tolstoy - **Man lives consciously for himself, but is an unconscious instrument in the attainment of the historical aims of humanity**
- Butterfield - **There is something in the nature of historical events which twists the course of history in a direction no man intended**
	- After a hundred years of minor local wars, we've had two back to back major wars since 1914. This does not mean that few individuals wanted peace.
	- It is difficult to believe that any individual willed the Great Depression of the 1930s.
- No individual wanted the above events, but these events were result of a series of human actions
- Therefore, history must consider the intentions behind actions to truly uncover the truth
- History can be written on the basis of **explanation in terms of human intentions**, of why in their mind they acted the way they did

#### Individual vs. Society
- The popular image of the individual in revolt against society is showing the same incorrect distinction between society and the individual
- **The individual himself is a product of society and its actions**
- No society is homogeneous; every society is filled with societal conflicts and the individuals who participate in those conflicts are by definition acting with society
- Both monarchs and rebel are products of **the same society, under different starting conditions.**
- Nietzsche - German radical, violently opposed the society back then. But he is also a product of the same society. Other societies would not have been able to produce such a radical.
- **The individual is an expression of the social forces acted upon him**
- The Great Man theory of history has gone out of fashion because of this realisation.
	- The actions of great figures would not have been so impactful if they had happened in a different society at a different time.
	- Tolstoy - Great men are labels giving names to events
	- The great man is representative of the existing forces and its effects on them. He is simultaneously the agent and the product of the historical process

#### Conclusion
- History is a social process
	- Individuals are engaged as social beings
	- There is no distinction between the individual and the society
- The reciprocal between the past and present is not between isolated individuals, but rather between societies (that of the past and of the present)
- Dual function of history - Enable man to understand the society of the past and increase his mastery over the society of the present.

### 3. History, Science and Morality
#### History and Science
- At the end of the 18th century, humanity began viewing science as one of the best tools to understand his environment
- People began wondering if the tools of science could also be used to analyse society and its actions. Thus conceived was the field of the social sciences
- Throughout the 19th century, the scientific method were applied to the view of society
	- With the creation of the Newtonian tradition, society was thought of as a mechanism.
	- With Darwin's revolution brought about by the discovery of evolution, science began to concern itself with the process of change and dynamics. These methods were also brought into the study of society, viewing society as a dynamic organism.
	- The Positivist approach to history fit in this view. History began to be viewed as a science.
- But with the increase in popularity of Collingwood's view of history as something dependent on interpretation, science itself underwent a similar revolution, with the increase in the significance of events over facts, especially in modern physics and astronomy.

#### The History of Science
- Scientists believed that the laws of nature - Newton's Law of Gravitation, Boyle's Law, etc - are definite and the job of the scientist is to discover similar definite laws
- Social Sciences tried to adopt a similar pattern - to find laws that hold true for anything in their domain (Adam Smith's Laws of the Market for economics)
- Buckle - **The course of human affairs is permeated by one glorious principle of universal and undeviating regularity**, i.e, history repeats itself
- Such a statement sounds similarly old fashioned to the scientist as it does to the historian
	- Henri Poincare - The propositions of science are hypotheses meant to encourage further structured growth in the field and are not immutable.
	- Modern scientists do not work to discover immutable laws, rather they try to create more accurate models and hypotheses which can be further built upon. It is essentially reciprocal or circular.
- All thinking happens on the grounds of some basic assumptions, which are themselves subject to revision based on that thinking. The hypotheses may be valid in some contexts and invalid in others.
- This equally fits the modern historian, who has abandoned his search for universal laws (or facts) and is now inquiring how things work.
- The meaning and use of a hypothesis in history is similar to the same in science. 

#### Hypothesis in History
- The periodization of history is also a hypothesis, valid as long as it provides some new insights and can be challenged upon.
- Similar is the division of history based on geographical sectors.
- This is a far cry from the 19th century positivist view of history as a collection of facts and their hope that this collection would be sufficient to settle all disputes
- **History advances from one fragmentary hypothesis to another**, isolating their facts through interpretations and testing their interpretations through facts.
- Barraclough - **History is a series of accepted judgements**
- But history is not a science
	- It deals with the unique, whereas science deals with the general
	- History teaches no lessons
	- History is unpredictable
	- History is necessarily subjective
	- History involves religion and morality
- The above points are not necessarily correct as will be seen below.

#### History Deals with the Unique
- Hobbes - "Nothing in the world is universal but names, for the things named are every one of them individual and singular"
- No two things in the natural sciences are unique either.
- Similarly no two event in history are identical. The historian, by his very use of language, commits to generalisation.
- The historian is interested in **what is general in the unique.** 
	- For example, the differences in the causes of different wars.
	- They all involve generalisations about the cause of war. The historian uses this generalisation to test his evidence
- History thrives on generalisations.
- Marx - **Events strikingly similar, but occurring in a different historical milieu, lead to completely dissimilar results. By studying each of these evolution separately and then comparing them, it is easy to find the key to the understanding of this phenomenon ; but it is never possible to arrive at this understanding by using the passe-partout of some historical-philosophical theory whose great virtue is to stand above history.**
- History is concerned with the **relation between the unique and the general**.

#### History teaches no lessons
- Generalisation, by definition, is to apply the lessons drawn from one set of events to another,.
- Those who reject generalisation also reject that anything can be learned from history.
- There are multiple contradictions to the statement that history teaches nothing
	- The lessons drawn in western Europe in the 17th, 18th and 19th centuries, from the history of the Old Testament, like its effects on the English Puritan revolution
	- The leaders of the Russian Revolution learnt from the histories of the French Revolution and the Spring of Nations
- **To learn about the present in the light of the past is also to learn about the past in the light of the present.**
- **History promotes a deeper understanding of both the past and the present through inter-relation between them**

#### History is Unpredictable
- It is said that unlike science, history cannot predict the future.
- But the problem with this statement lies within science itself
- The laws of sciences are **statements of tendency**, statements of what will happen in normal circumstances
- Science cannot deal with outliers, but that does not make its principles unworthy of study
- **Modern physical theories deal only with probabilities**
- The question of predictions in history lies in the distinction between the general and the specific.
	- The historian through his tendency to generalise, gives general guides for future actions but cannot use them to predict specifics, since it is unique.
- The historian also works on possibilities, analysing events that are similar to the event in inquiry and gives a general guide on what can occur, which is realised only in the event's occurrence.
	- But this does not mean the drawn general guide is worthless or invalid.

#### History is necessarily Subjective
- Since social sciences involve the study of humans by humans, subjectivity cannot be removed.
- The point of view of the historian is present in all his observations
- **History is defined through relativity**
- **It is also true that the process of observation affects and modifies what is being observed**
	- The human beings in question may be aware of the analysis being done and be induced by it to change their behavior
	- This is why people who are aware of history, rarely repeat history, since they know its methods of analysis and inquiry.
		- The Bolsheviks knew that the French Revolution ended with Napoleon, therefore mistrusted Trotsky, who most resembled Napoleon and put their trust in Stalin instead.
	- This process may work in the converse direction as well. The historian's strong belief in an idea may itself result in its materialisation (a self fulfilling prophecy)
- Modern science also involves some principles of relativity and the effect of the observer.
	- All measurements are subject to variations due to the impossibility of isolating the observer from the object in question
- Science also gives different priorities to different areas.

#### History involves Religion and Morality
- To be an astronomer is incompatible with the belief that the motion of the celestial bodies is the result of divine will.
- Similarly, to be a historian is incompatible with the belief of divine intervention in the activities of humanity. He cannot invoke God as explanation for historical events.
- The historian must solve his problems without involving divine will as the answer.
- The relation of history to morality is more complication
	- The historian is not required to pass moral judgements about the characters in his writing.
	- If the morality of a person had **little to no significant consequences to the course of history or the question under inquiry**, then the historian is free to not include morality into his work. (**The historian selects the facts**)
- The question of morality in history arises when we try to apply moral judgements.
	- Acton - **The inflexibility of the moral code is the secret of the authority, the dignity and the utility of history**, reflects his belief in the objectivity and supreme significance of historical facts
	- Prof. Knowles - **The historian is not a judge, still less a hanging judge**, reflects the fact that the historian may not always be unbiased when evaluating the morality of certain figures.
- Historical facts always involve some form of interpretation, and that interpretation is not free of moral or value judgements.
	- A historian can condemn the institution of slavery, without passing judgement on the individual slave owner.
- **History is a process of struggle**, where certain results are achieved by certain groups at the expense of others. **The losers pay**
	- Every great period has it causalities as well as its victories and there is no universal tool to measure them against each other.
	- The retention of custom is as turbulent as innovation. The cost of conservation falls just as heavily on the under-privileged as the cost of innovation on those deprived of their privileges.
	- This issue is most prominent in periods of radical change, and the nature of the historian is most visible in these works.
		- Most historians describe the industrial revolution as a great achievement. They may also talk about the exploitation of the working classes that was required for this achievement
	- **Those who pay the cost are rarely those who reap the benefits**
		- Historians condone the colonisation of Asia and Africa on the ground not only of its immediate effects but also because of its long term consequences
		- Modern India is the child of British rule, but the freedom fighters did not get to experience this modernity.
- The abstract concepts of morality are indispensable, but are devoid of meaning until they are applied.
- Those who try to find a universal standard for moral judgement cannot do so through history
	- The attempt to construct such a standard contradicts the very essence of history
	- History is filled with change and so is morality
	- History compares different societies in a relative manner, not using absolutes like good and bad.
	- The emergence of a certain value can be tracked to historical conditions
	- **Every group has their own system of values, created by their own histories, which they use to pass judgement of their actions, and each system will try to defend itself from values that seem alien or inconvenient.**
- **The absolute value is as much of an illusion as the absolute individual**
