Gun Island: a Bengali legend, a climate migration, and the novel Ghosh said nobody could write

Amitav Ghosh · novel, 2019 · sequel to The Hungry Tide (2004). Your PDF is the 295-page ebook. It has no printed page numbers, so every "p." below is the PDF page number (the number your PDF reader shows). The novel runs pp. 9–291.

In one line: A sceptical Brooklyn rare-book dealer, Deen Datta, chases a forgotten 17th-century legend about a merchant who fled the snake goddess Manasa Devi, and finds that the legend's route (Bengal to Venice, driven by the droughts and storms of the Little Ice Age) is being repeated today by climate-displaced Bengali migrants, while snakes, spiders, dolphins and whales are also on the move because of warming.

Short on time? Do this

If you have 30 minutes, learn these five things in this order:

  1. The 60-second summary below. It opens almost any answer on this novel.
  2. The decoding of the legend (table in "Key terms"): Bonduki Sadagar = "the Merchant who went to Venice", not "Gun Merchant". The fantasy place-names are real places hit by the 17th-century climate crisis. This is the novel's big idea: old stories carry real climate history.
  3. Three themes: (a) climate migration, of people and animals; (b) the improbable, i.e. coincidence, "just a story", and Deen's "faith in chance"; (c) possession, Cinta's claim that the modern world has lost its will in front of the crisis.
  4. Eight quotes: "war between profit and Nature" (14); "Even the animals are moving" (66); "Politics, religion and sex" (68); "No one knows where they belong any more, neither humans nor animals" (103); "Only through stories can… silent beings speak to us" (133); "chance was the very foundation of reality" (189); "the world of today presents all the symptoms of demonic possession" (221); "eating the foundations of the city" (235).
  5. The Great Derangement link (its own section below). In GD Ghosh says the realist novel banishes the improbable, so it cannot handle climate change. Gun Island is his attempt to write the novel he said was missing.

The 60-second summary

In Gun Island (2019), Amitav Ghosh returns to the Sundarbans of The Hungry Tide and then follows its people across the world. The narrator, Dinanath "Deen" Datta, a rationalist rare-book dealer in Brooklyn, hears of a shrine to the Bonduki Sadagar ("Gun Merchant"), a merchant who fled the snake goddess Manasa Devi (pp. 11–23). At the shrine, a king cobra bites the teenager Tipu, who then has visions, including one that predicts the death of Piya's dolphin Rani (pp. 82–99). Back in the US, Deen meets wildfires in Los Angeles and a venomous sea snake that has moved north with warming water (pp. 120–140). His friend, the Venetian historian Cinta, decodes the legend: "Bonduk" is Venice, and the merchant's route ran through Egypt, Istanbul and Venice during the droughts, fires and plagues of the Little Ice Age (pp. 141–148).

In Part Two, Venice (pp. 155–291), Deen finds the Sundarbans boy Rafi working illegally among thousands of Bengali migrants. Tipu and Rafi had left together, driven out by salinity, storms and the dying delta, and crossed India, Pakistan, Iran and Turkey (pp. 240–247). Venice itself is sinking, flooded and eaten by shipworms (pp. 169, 234–236), and a brown recluse spider turns up in Cinta's flat (pp. 201, 208–209). The novel ends at sea off Sicily: a refugee "Blue Boat" carrying Tipu is surrounded by whales, dolphins, glowing water and a "storm" of migrating birds, and an admiral rescues the refugees by calling it a "miracle" (pp. 277–289). Cinta dies the same night (p. 291). Piya insists everything has "a scientific explanation" (p. 288); the novel leaves both readings open. Its argument is that climate change makes the world feel uncanny and improbable, that people and animals are migrating for the same reasons, and that old stories hold knowledge that the modern idea of "chance" has trained us to ignore.

Plot step by step

Each step gives what happens, the page, and the theme it carries. The bold line is what to remember.

  1. A word starts it: bundook (pp. 9–25)

    Deen, near sixty, lonely, "rational, secular", once wrote a PhD on the Manasa Devi legend of Chand Sadagar. Kanai Dutt (from The Hungry Tide) asks if he has heard of Bonduki Sadagar. Kanai places the shrine in the Sundarbans because that is "the frontier where commerce and the wilderness look each other directly in the eye" (14). Nilima Bose heard of the shrine after the 1970 Bhola cyclone (18–20): the shrine's bell rang before the storm and saved a whole village. It is cared for by a Muslim boatman, revered by Hindus (Manasa) and Muslims (a pir called Ilyas) (21). The legend must never be written down (21). Nilima wants it recorded because the islands are "being swallowed up by the sea" (24).

    • Remember: the novel opens with a storm, a legend and a disappearing coastline.

  2. Cinta and the "supernatural" (pp. 26–52)

    Deen dates the shrine to 1605–1690 from a riddle-couplet (27–28). A random call from Cinta in Venice nudges him to go. Flashback: Deen scorned a jatra about Manasa as superstition; Cinta told him the Inquisition invented the words "superstition" and "supernatural" (41), and spoke of de Martino and tarantism. Cinta's husband and daughter Lucia died in a car crash; she heard Lucia's voice before the accident (45–48). Deen's first love Durga, a Naxalite, was killed in an "encounter" (49–50).

    • Remember: the rationalist vs. the believer is set up early. Cinta argues the line between natural and supernatural is a historical invention.

  3. Aila and the "people-moving industry" (pp. 53–70)

    Moyna explains Cyclone Aila (2009): few deaths but lasting ruin, as embankments broke and salt water made land "uncultivable for a generation" (54). Young people leave; traffickers swarm; "both land and water were turning against those who lived in the Sundarbans" (55). Tipu (Moyna's son, once "Tutul") is a smuggler's agent: fish catch down, land turning salty, "Even the animals are moving" (66). "The Internet is the migrants' magic carpet" (67). He coaches asylum seekers to tell stories of persecution, because Europe will not hear of floods or arsenic: "Politics, religion and sex is what they're looking for" (68).

    • Remember: climate causes are real but not legally recognised, so migrants have to invent other stories. This is a key point for climate-migration questions.

  4. The shrine and the cobra (pp. 71–84)

    "It's the river that's moved" (71): the shrine is now on the water's edge. Deen crawls through mud. The temple is Bishnupur style. Rafi's late mother traced the eroded friezes. Rafi explains them: cowries, "Land of Palm Sugar Candy", "Land of Kerchieves", an island within an island (Gun Island) (79–80). A king cobra rears behind Deen. Tipu throws a net and is bitten (82). Rafi sucks the venom and swallows some (83).

  5. Visions and Rani (pp. 85–108)

    In delirium, Tipu sees "shadows" kept off by snakes, knows Rafi's real name is Ilyas (88), and says to warn Piya about "Rani". At that moment Piya gets a tracker alert (94). Rani is an Irrawaddy dolphin whose pod has been pushed upriver by rising salinity (98) and possibly by a refinery's effluents (101–102). The pod is found beached on Garjontola (104). Piya: "We're in a new world now. No one knows where they belong any more, neither humans nor animals" (103). She rejects Deen's talk of "visions" (105).

    • Remember: human migrants and dolphins are pushed by the same changes. Piya always gives the scientific reading.

  6. Brooklyn: haunted (pp. 109–119)

    Deen feels something "ancient" has entered him, "memory itself, except that it was not my own" (109). Tipu sends chat questions: does bhuta mean ghost? Deen: from bhu, "to be", so a being, but also "the past"; Tipu: "the past was present in the present?" (110). On shamans; on greed: "it's not parasites we got inside of us, it's greed!" (117). Piya's friend Lisa warned a town about bark beetles and wildfire and was then blamed as a witch (114–115).

  7. Los Angeles: fire and sea snakes (pp. 120–140)

    Deen's plane lands through wildfire smoke; a raptor drops a snake in mid-air and Deen screams "Snake!" and is detained (122–124). A young historian lectures on the Little Ice Age: famine, war, plague and talk of apocalypse in the 1600s, and the start of coal use (128–130). The museum is evacuated: "We've got to show Mother Nature that we're not quitters!" (131). Cinta: never say "just a story" (133). At Venice Beach a yellow-bellied sea snake kills Gisa's dog (137); Piya explains they are "migrating northwards" with warmer seas (140). The shrine has been washed away by a storm (140).

  8. The legend decoded (pp. 141–153)

    Cinta's lecture: the Venetian getto was a foundry; Arabic al-Bunduqeyya means Venice, and also guns, bullets and hazelnuts; so bundook is "Venetian" (142). Deen realises Bonduki Sadagar = "the Merchant who went to Venice" (143). Cinta decodes the rest (144–147): Taal-misri = Misr (Egypt); Rumaali = Rumeli (Istanbul), where the 1660 fire blamed the Jews; the sign beside Ilyas is a Hebrew aleph (Ilyas is a Portuguese Jew who fled the Inquisition in Goa). Cinta: "Ecco! You have your legend!" (147). Deen sinks into money anxiety in Brooklyn; then Gisa hires him as translator for a migrant documentary in Venice, where there are "many, many Bengalis" (152). From the air the Venetian lagoon looks like the Sundarbans (153).

    • Remember: the legend is a coded record of climate-driven migration in the 1600s.

  9. Venice of the migrants (pp. 156–179)

    In the Ghetto, Deen sits and imagines Manasa Devi not as a cruel goddess but as a portavoce, a "voice-carrier" between humans and snakes (157–158). A Bangla shout saves him from falling masonry: the worker is Rafi (159–160). Lubna lost family to a cyclone in Madaripur and a tree full of snakes (165). Bengalis make Venice run (167). Cinta's palazzo floods; its lobby is under water much of the time (169). Rafi recalls the iron gun-room and the spider bite in the legend (176). A thug in a green cap threatens Rafi. Gisa: the Blue Boat of refugees is heading from the Sinai; the minister refuses to let it land (177–179).

  10. Strandings, chance, and Bilal (pp. 180–202)

    Piya got an anonymous email predicting a mass dolphin beaching; it happened (181–184), and the footage may close the refinery. She finds Tipu faked his Bangalore photos and is somewhere between Turkey and Egypt (185–188). Deen clings to "chance" as his faith (189–190). Rafi was beaten for a debt he took to pay for Tipu's passage (195–196). Bilal tells of being tricked to Libya, enslaved, and losing his friend Kabir at sea; he sends money to Kabir's parents (196–200). A spider sits on Deen's laptop (201).

  11. Dreams, possession, La Salute (pp. 203–229)

    Palash, a Dhaka graduate, explains the scafisti and that Lubna's activist husband Munir died after crossing the Mafia (205–206). Piya's expert identifies the spider as a brown recluse, spreading north "because it's getting so much hotter in Europe" (208–209). Deen tries to open a case holding the Hypnerotomachia Poliphili, sure the Merchant saw Manasa's face in it (213). Cinta: the spider is "here because of our history" (219); the modern world shows "all the symptoms of demonic possession" (221). At Santa Maria della Salute, built after the 1630 plague, she shows him Unde origo inde salus (227–229).

  12. High water and shipworms (pp. 230–238)

    An artwork of a sea-monster; then at the Fondamente Nove, Cinta shows shipworms spreading with warmer lagoon water: "They are literally eating the foundations of the city" (235). The pier collapses; worms swarm over them (236); the flood rises to their chests; Bilal, squatting nearby, calls an ambulance (237–238).

  13. Rafi's story of the crossing (pp. 239–249)

    In hospital, Rafi says the flooded city is "like home – we're used to floods" (239). Tipu's seizures continued; he planned their escape and the fake Bangalore job (240–241). The journey: dalals, connection houses, "jackals" (coyotes), extortion, Tipu nearly raped and saved by a seizure (243), border shooting in the Iran-Turkey mountains where they were separated (245). Tipu dreamt of an Ethiopian woman, a "forishta", and went to Egypt (246). Rafi shows a newspaper photo: Tipu is on the Blue Boat (247). Deen joins Lubna's rescue ship.

  14. Winds and the Lucania (pp. 250–264)

    Hail, then a tornado crosses the road in front of the minibus (255–256). A robed, turbaned stranger shows them another road; Rafi: "It's him – Bonduki Sadagar" (256). Deen: just a Moroccan farm-worker (257). Cinta decodes the last riddle: Shikol-dwip = Sicily (Arabic Siqilliya) (252). Twisters over the lagoon; trees fall in Rome and Gisa says her family "have become refugees" (260). Piya opens up about Fokir's death (261).

  15. Sightings and the storm (pp. 265–291)

    Whale and dolphin species gather, all heading towards Sicily (266–281). News: a tornado in the Sinai had freed the captives in a trafficking "connection house", led by an Ethiopian woman, who took the Blue Boat with traffickers' data (267–268). Right-wing flotillas shout; Deen reads them as afraid of the reversal of colonial migration (283–285). A storm of millions of migrating birds, whirling dolphins and whales, and glowing water surround the Blue Boat (286–287). The admiral, an old Venetian Cinta knew, rescues the refugees and says "what we witnessed today was indeed a miracle" (288). Piya: "migratory patterns intersecting in an unusual way" (288). Piya invites Deen to Oregon (275). Cinta says Lucia has come to take her, and dies (290–291).

    • Remember: the ending repeats the legend's last scene: captives freed "by the creatures of the sky and sea" near Sicily.

Characters

Dinanath "Deen" Datta

Narrator. Bengali-American rare-book dealer in Brooklyn, PhD on Bengali folklore, proud rationalist. His arc: from "faith in chance" to a reluctant risveglio (awakening). He is the modern sceptical reader the novel works on.

Giacinta "Cinta" Schiavon

Venetian historian of the Inquisition and spice trade. Decodes the legend. Voice of stories, history and the "possession" argument. Grieves her daughter Lucia; dies at the end.

Piya Roy

Cetologist from The Hungry Tide. Studies Irrawaddy dolphins; fights a refinery. Always offers the scientific explanation. Guilt about Fokir, Tipu's father.

Tipu (Tutul)

Moyna and Fokir's son. Americanised, hacker, migrant-smuggling agent. Bitten by the cobra, gets visions. Becomes a migrant himself; ends on the Blue Boat.

Rafi (Ilyas)

Grandson of the shrine's Muslim caretaker. Sundarbans fisherman, knows the old ways. Tipu's partner. Works illegally in Venice; reads the legend's signs.

Nilima, Moyna, Horen, Kanai

All from The Hungry Tide. Nilima runs the Badabon Trust; Moyna is a nurse; Horen is a boatman who measures time by cyclones (60); Kanai sets the plot going.

Lubna Alam ("Lubna-khala")

Bangladeshi in Venice who helps migrants. Lost family to a cyclone in Madaripur. Widow of Munir, an activist killed after crossing the Mafia.

Bilal and Palash

Two migrant stories. Bilal: land dispute, Libya, slavery, lost friend at sea. Palash: middle-class graduate who chased a "Finland" dream and is now stuck.

Gisa, Admiral Vigonovo, the scafista

Gisa: Cinta's niece, documentary maker. The admiral: rescues the refugees and calls it a "miracle". The green-capped scafista: trafficker tied to the Mob.

Non-human characters

King cobra, Rani and the dolphins, the yellow-bellied sea snake, the brown recluse spider, shipworms, migrating birds and whales. They act and they move. Treat them as characters in your answers.

Manasa Devi

Bengali goddess of snakes. Deen re-reads her as a portavoce, a translator between humans and snakes. If humans ignore her, profit "would recognize no restraint" towards other living things (157–158).

Chand Sadagar

The classic merchant who defied Manasa; his son Lakhindar dies of snakebite, saved by Behula (12). The Gun Merchant is a local variant.

Dhaam

Shrine. Bishnupur-style brick temple, 1605–1690, shared by Hindus and Muslims (21, 28, 74). Washed away by a storm (140).

Little Ice Age

17th-century global cooling with drought, famine, war, plague and apocalyptic fear (128–130). The "general crisis" that pushed the Merchant out (147).

Bhuta

From Sanskrit bhu, "to be". A being, and also the past. So "the past was present in the present" (110). At the end, the birds are "a storm of living beings, bhutas" (286).

Bauley

A man who leads people into the Sundarbans jungle with a special bond to animals; Rafi's grandfather was one (112).

Dalal, connection house, jackals, scafista

The migration industry: brokers (67), holding houses (198, 242), border guides or coyotes (242), Italian traffickers who trap migrants in debt (205).

Possession / risveglio

Cinta: Inquisition cases were about losing "will and freedom" (220). Today we live among "impersonal systems" and are powerless before the crisis, so the world shows "symptoms of demonic possession" (221). Deen's unease is a risveglio, an awakening (222).

Aila and Bhola

Cyclone Aila 2009 (54) and the Bhola cyclone 1970 (19). Aila killed few but salted the land and drove people out.

Acqua alta and shipworms

Venice's high-water floods (169, 234) and shipworms that warmer water lets in, hollowing the city's wooden piles (235).

Unde origo inde salus

"From the origin salvation comes" (229). Deen reads it as: "our deliverance lies not in the future but in the past" (291).

Stormo

Italian for a flock in flight. Deen: the right word for the "storm of living beings" at the end (286).

Links to other course texts

Points marked from memory come from general knowledge of that text, not from your project PDFs. Check them against your notes.

The Hungry Tide (Ghosh)

same place, same people · strongest comparison
  • Sequel: Piya, Kanai, Nilima, Moyna, Horen return; Fokir died in the storm that saved Piya (261); Tipu is his son.
  • Legend + science: HT pairs the Bon Bibi legend with Piya's dolphin science from memory; GI pairs the Manasa Devi legend with the same science. In both, local knowledge (Fokir, Rafi, Horen) sees what instruments do not.
  • Shift in scale: HT stays in the delta (conservation vs. settlers, Morichjhapi from memory). GI shows the delta's people forced out into the world, so climate change globalises the Sundarbans' problems.
  • Dolphins: in HT the Irrawaddy dolphins are a mystery to study; in GI they beach themselves, displaced by salinity (98) and effluents (102).

Use when: "compare Ghosh's two Sundarbans novels", "human and non-human", "local knowledge vs science" (midsem Q1 style).

Rob Nixon, Slow Violence

concept to apply
  • Slow violence in GI: Aila's salt water ruining land "for a generation" (54); refinery effluents, fish kills and a crab die-off (102, 114); shipworms hollowing Venice from inside, solid outside, "probably hollow" inside (235). These are gradual, dispersed and easy to ignore.
  • Representation problem: Tipu says asylum systems want "a story of persecution" (68), not floods or arsenic. Slow environmental harm does not count as a reason to flee. This is Nixon's point that attritional violence lacks a recognisable story.
  • Who suffers: Dalit Tipu, fisher Rafi, villagers in Madaripur. The "environmentalism of the poor" Nixon's phrase, from memory.
  • Contrast: Ghosh's novel stages spectacle (tornado, cobra, bird storm). Deen even says reason fails in "fleeting instants of catastrophe", not "the long, slow duration of everyday time" (228). Argue that Ghosh uses spectacle to make slow violence visible, or critique this as the opposite of Nixon.

Use when: "apply slow violence to a text", "gradual and dispersed harm".

Jamieson / Heise, Anthropocene: Love It or Leave It

agency
  • Jamieson sees in the Anthropocene "a widespread sense of the loss of agency" (Jamieson 15). Cinta's "possession" argument is the same diagnosis in a novelist's language: "Everybody knows . . . and yet we are powerless" (221).
  • Jamieson's confusion between causation and complicity matches Cinta's "you have a prior connection with that spider, whether you like it or not" (219).
  • Jamieson's respect for nature as partner matches Manasa as portavoce (157).

Use when: "agency", "ethics of the Anthropocene".

Arundhati Roy, The Cost of Living

development vs displacement
  • Roy: people displaced by dams "don't count" (Roy 4). GI: climate migrants don't count as refugees (68), and are "illegal" in Europe.
  • Piya's fight against the refinery, smeared as "foreign agents" (101), parallels the anti-dam movement Roy defends.
  • Difference: Roy names a clear culprit (the State, Big Dams); in GI the cause is diffuse, "systems" that "did not obey any human master" (285).

River of Stories (Orijit Sen)

myth beside realism
  • Both braid myth (Malgu gayan's creation songs / the Gun Merchant panchali) with present-day reportage, and both treat myth as knowledge, not decoration.
  • Both have an urban outsider (journalist Vishnu / Deen) who learns from marginalised people (Relku / Rafi, Tipu).

The Rime of the Ancient Mariner (Coleridge)

midsem text
  • Both: a man's disrespect for non-human life brings a curse Rime, from memory. The Merchant refuses Manasa; the Mariner shoots the albatross.
  • The turn in both comes through snakes: the Mariner blesses the water-snakes "unaware" and the curse lifts from memory; in GI snakes are Manasa's messengers and Tipu feels them protecting him (87).
  • Sea creatures and birds decide the fate of a ship in both. GI's end, with birds over a drifting boat, inverts the Mariner's albatross.
  • Both narrators are compelled to tell their story (Deen writes this one; the Mariner stops wedding guests).

Pterodactyl, Puran Sahay and Pirtha (Mahasweta Devi)

midsem text
  • A non-human creature appears as a messenger of loss: the pterodactyl from memory, the cobra and the dolphins in GI.
  • An educated outsider (journalist Puran / book dealer Deen) is forced to take indigenous or folk knowledge seriously.
  • Drought, famine and displacement in Pirtha from memory match the Merchant's drought (61) and the Sundarbans' exodus (55).

How Gun Island answers The Great Derangement

This is the most likely essay question. GD page refs are from your GD guide (Part I only, pp. 3–37).

What Ghosh argued in GD (2016)

  • Climate change is missing from serious fiction; it is "a crisis of culture, and thus of the imagination" (GD 9).
  • "Probability and the modern novel are in fact twins" (GD 16); the novel banished "the improbable" (GD 17).
  • His own 1978 Delhi tornado would look like "a contrivance of last resort" in a novel (GD 16).
  • Climate events reveal "nonhuman interlocutors" (GD 30) and are our own work "returning to haunt us" (GD 32).
  • But they must not be treated as fantasy: they "are actually happening on this earth, at this time" (GD 27).

What Gun Island does (2019)

  • Fills the novel with the improbable: the cobra, Tipu's visions, the falling snake, meeting Rafi in the Ghetto, the spider, Bilal at the right moment, the stranger after the tornado.
  • Puts tornadoes in the plot: in the Veneto (255–256) and the Sinai (268). The thing GD said a novel could not use.
  • Stages the probability debate: Deen clings to "faith in chance" (189–190); Cinta refuses "just a story" (133).
  • Gives non-human actors: snakes, spiders, dolphins, shipworms, birds. Cinta: "only through stories can… silent beings speak to us" (133).
  • Keeps it real: every uncanny event has a climate science explanation through Piya (140, 209, 235, 288–289). So it is not fantasy, matching GD 27.
  • Adds migration: the novel ties climate to the movement of people and animals across borders.

Ghosh's Parts II–III of GD (politics, Asia and migration) are not in your PDF. If you mention them, say so carefully. from memory

Strengths and limits

Strengths

  • Makes climate migration personal: Tipu, Rafi, Bilal, Lubna, Palash each have a distinct story and cause.
  • Links human and animal displacement as one process (66, 103, 140, 209).
  • Gives deep history: the Little Ice Age shows climate crisis, migration and apocalyptic fear are not new (128–130, 147).
  • Connects the Global South and North: Venice floods too; Rome's Gisa becomes a "refugee" (260).
  • Takes folk knowledge seriously without rejecting science: Piya and Cinta both stand.
  • Reads the xenophobic backlash as fear of losing colonial privilege (283–285).

Limits (good for "critically evaluate")

  • Coincidences can feel contrived; the "miracle" ending may read as a deus ex machina. Ghosh himself said this risk exists (GD 16).
  • Characters often lecture: the keynote, Cinta, Palash. Ideas sometimes come before story.
  • Narrated by a privileged, US-based man; the migrants' voices reach us through him, as translator.
  • The ending relies on nature and an admiral's conscience, not on migrants' own politics or collective action. (Though the Ethiopian woman leads the escape, 268.)
  • Spectacle may undercut the slow, invisible harm Nixon asks us to see.
  • Piya's science and Cinta's mysticism are both left open; some readers find this undecided.

Likely 10-mark questions

Modelled on your midsem (any 3 of 6, 10 marks, "substantiate with evidence"). Aim for: thesis, 3 or 4 evidence paragraphs with page numbers, one cross-text link, a counterpoint, a conclusion.

Q1In The Great Derangement, Ghosh argues that the modern novel cannot accommodate the improbable. How does Gun Island respond to this challenge?

Thesis

Gun Island is Ghosh's attempt to write the novel he said was missing: it builds its plot from improbable events, but ties each one to real climate science, so the uncanny becomes a way of showing a changed planet.

Points

  1. GD's claim: probability and the novel are "twins"; the improbable is banished (GD 16–17).
  2. GI fills its plot with improbable events: cobra (82), Tipu's prediction of Rani's death (94), the Ghetto near-miss and Rafi (159–160), tornadoes (255, 268), the bird storm (286).
  3. It stages the debate inside the novel: Deen's "faith in chance" (189–190) vs Cinta's "never say just a story" (133–134).
  4. Non-human interlocutors (GD 30): snakes, spiders, dolphins "speak" through stories (133).
  5. Not fantasy: Piya explains sea snakes (140), the brown recluse (209), migrations (288–289). This satisfies GD 27.

Counterpoint

The miracle ending risks becoming the "contrivance of last resort" (GD 16) that Ghosh warned about.

Conclusion

The novel does not abandon realism; it widens it, so that in a warming world "nothing is surprising now" (289).

Q2Discuss Gun Island as a novel of climate migration. How does it link human and non-human movement?

Thesis

The novel presents migration as an ecological event: people and animals are pushed by the same changes, and the borders that stop humans mean nothing to snakes, spiders and whales.

Points

  1. Push factors in the Sundarbans: Aila's salt (54), no fish, "land's turning salty" (66), Rafi's grandfather told him he would have to leave (92).
  2. Human journeys: Tipu and Rafi (241–247), Bilal (196–200), Lubna's cyclone (165).
  3. Animal journeys: Rani's pod upriver (98), sea snakes northward (140), brown recluse (209), shipworms (235), whales and birds (281, 286). "Even the animals are moving" (66); "neither humans nor animals" (103).
  4. Climate migrants unrecognised: they need stories of "politics, religion and sex" (68).
  5. History: the Merchant's 17th-century flight from drought and storms (61, 147). "The past was present in the present" (110).

Link

Nixon: slow harms lack a recognised narrative. Roy: the displaced "don't count".

Conclusion

The Blue Boat's rescue by animals and a sympathetic admiral imagines solidarity across species and borders.

Q3What role does the legend of the Gun Merchant and Manasa Devi play in the novel? Discuss myth as a form of environmental knowledge.

Thesis

The legend is a coded historical record of climate-driven migration and a moral warning about profit that ignores the non-human.

Points

  1. The legend's calamities (drought, floods, snakes) match the Little Ice Age (61, 128–130).
  2. The decoding: Venice, Egypt, Istanbul, Sicily (142–147, 252). The fantasy is geography.
  3. Manasa as portavoce: without her, profit "would recognize no restraint" (157–158). The merchant represents commerce at war with Nature (14).
  4. Oral, unwritten, revived in crises (13, 21, 27). Cinta: stories let "silent beings speak" (133).
  5. The legend repeats: iron room and spider (176, 201), the Island of Chains and the miracle at sea (252, 286).

Link

Hungry Tide (Bon Bibi), River of Stories (Malgu's songs).

Counterpoint

Deen and Piya stay sceptical; the novel never proves the legend causes anything.

Q4Using Rob Nixon's concept of slow violence, analyse Gun Island. What are the strengths and limits of the framework for this novel?

Thesis

Slow violence explains the novel's causes (salinity, effluents, warming water) but not its form, which relies on sudden spectacle.

Points

  1. Slow violence: Aila's lasting salt damage (54–55), refinery leaks and fish kills (102, 114), shipworms (235), Rani's shrinking pod (98).
  2. Invisibility: asylum law needs persecution stories (68); Lisa's warning ignored and turned against her (115).
  3. Victims are poor and marginalised: Dalit Tipu, fisherman Rafi, Bilal.
  4. Limit: the novel's key moments are fast (tornado, cobra, bird storm). Deen says reason fails in "fleeting instants of catastrophe" (228).

Conclusion

Ghosh may use fast, uncanny events to draw attention to slow causes. Argue whether this solves or betrays Nixon's representational problem.

Q5Compare Gun Island and The Hungry Tide in their representation of the Sundarbans and the relation between science and local knowledge.

Points

  1. Same place and people; Fokir's death (261) links them.
  2. Piya as science in both; local knowers: Fokir in HT, Rafi and Horen in GI (Rafi hears worms like crabs in embankments, 240; spots whales first, 265).
  3. HT: conflict inside the delta (tigers vs people) from memory. GI: the delta's people expelled into the world.
  4. Both use legend (Bon Bibi / Manasa Devi) as a counterweight to science.
  5. Change: in GI, Piya's science also fails to explain everything (105, 288–289).
Q6Discuss Cinta's claim that "the world of today presents all the symptoms of demonic possession." How does the novel explore agency and inaction?

Points

  1. The argument: Inquisition cases were about losing "will and freedom" (220); today "impersonal systems" make presence unnecessary (221).
  2. "Everybody knows . . . and yet we are powerless" (221): inaction despite knowledge.
  3. Examples: the museum director's "not quitters" (131); Deen's insurance spiral (149–150); Tipu on greed (117); systems "inscrutable as demons" (285).
  4. Deen's breakdown reframed as risveglio, awakening (222).
  5. Agency returns at the end: the admiral acts on "the law of humanity" (289).

Link

Jamieson's "loss of agency" (15).

Q7Venice and the Sundarbans mirror each other in Gun Island. Discuss.

Points

  1. From the air, the lagoon looks like the Sundarbans (153).
  2. Both built on mud, eaten from inside: crabs vs shipworms (240, 235); both flood (169, 234).
  3. Rafi: "For us it's like home – we're used to floods" (239). Bengalis run flooded Venice.
  4. Both historic hubs of trade and migration: cowries, spices, the Ghetto (147–148, 225).
  5. The rich city is also vulnerable: "so completely at the mercy of the earth" (237).

Conclusion

Climate change erases the line between "developed" and "vulnerable" places.

Q8How does Gun Island represent non-human agency? Compare with The Rime of the Ancient Mariner.

Points

  1. The cobra protects the shrine and "would not have harmed anybody" if not attacked (82).
  2. Rani's gaze of "gratitude" (98) and Piya's refusal to name it (113).
  3. The spider seems to look at Deen (201, 219); worms test "whether we were real" (236).
  4. The bird storm as a "limb of the earth" (286).
  5. Rime: albatross, water-snakes, the sea as moral actors from memory. Both turn on respect for despised creatures.

Counterpoint

Piya's reading: animals respond to physical change, not to humans (288–289).

Exam tips and traps

  • The narrator is Deen, not Ghosh. Write "Deen reflects…" or "the narrator…".
  • Bonduki Sadagar is first translated "Gun Merchant" but means "the Merchant who went to Venice". Mention both.
  • Cite as Amitav Ghosh, Gun Island (2019). Say your page numbers are from the course PDF if you want to be safe.
  • Don't call the ending simply supernatural. Give both readings: the admiral's "miracle" and Piya's "migratory patterns intersecting" (288).
  • Use Piya as your counterpoint in any essay that leans on the uncanny, and Cinta as the counterpoint in any essay that leans on science.
  • Don't mix up the cyclones: Bhola 1970 (the shrine saves villagers), Aila 2009 (salt, exodus).
  • Always add one non-human example (snake, spider, dolphin, shipworm, birds) to keep the answer environmental.
  • Structure: two-line thesis, one quote with page per paragraph, one cross-text link (GD first), a counterpoint, and a conclusion that answers the exact question.
