The Hungry Tide: tigers, dolphins, refugees, and who gets to live in the tide country

Amitav Ghosh · novel, 2004 · prequel to Gun Island (2019). Your PDF is the 325-page Penguin Canada ebook. It has no printed page numbers, so every "p." below is the PDF page number (the number your PDF reader shows). The novel runs pp. 6–323; the Author's Note is p. 324.

In one line: Two outsiders, Piya (an American-Indian marine biologist hunting rare dolphins) and Kanai (a Delhi translator reading his dead uncle's notebook), enter the Sundarbans with Fokir, an illiterate local fisherman; the novel sets conservation (save the tiger and the dolphin) against survival (the poor who live and die there), through the 1979 Morichjhãpi massacre, a tiger burnt alive, and a cyclone that kills Fokir.

Part One · The Ebb: Bhata · pp. 6–142

Arrivals. Piya hires Fokir and finds the dolphins' pool. Kanai reaches Lusibari and starts reading Nirmal's 1979 notebook about Morichjhãpi and Kusum. Stories of Hamilton, Bon Bibi and the refugees.

Part Two · The Flood: Jowar · pp. 143–323

Rising stakes. All four sail on the Megha. The notebook reaches the siege and massacre. The tiger is killed (236–241). Kanai is "judged" at Garjontola. The cyclone kills Fokir (309–315). Piya stays to build a project named after him.

Short on time? Do this

If you have 30 minutes, learn these five things in this order:

  1. The 60-second summary below. It opens almost any answer on this novel.
  2. The central conflict: conservation vs. survival. Two scenes carry it. Kusum's speech during the Morichjhãpi siege ("Who are these people… who love animals so much that they are willing to kill us for them?", p. 215) and the Piya–Kanai argument after the tiger is burnt ("people like you… without regard for the human costs" vs. "it'll be people next", pp. 245–246).
  3. Four ways of living in the Sundarbans, one per character: Piya = science/conservation; Kanai = language, the urban elite; Fokir = lived, bodily local knowledge; Nirmal = revolutionary idealism and poetry; Nilima = slow, practical reform. (Use any two for midsem-style Q1.)
  4. Eight quotes: "the boundaries between land and water are always mutating" (10); "hostility to their presence" (10); "the wild and the sown… held in careful balance" (84); "transformation is the rule of life" (183); "worth less than dirt or dust" (215); "too poor to matter" (245); "people like you… without regard for the human costs" (246); "home is where the Orcaella are" (323).
  5. Slow violence + environmental injustice links: Morichjhãpi = displacement in the name of conservation; weekly tiger deaths "never reported" (245) = Nixon's invisible, attritional violence; cyclones and salinity = the slow-and-sudden mix. Compare with Pterodactyl (tribals displaced by "development").

The 60-second summary

In The Hungry Tide (2004), Amitav Ghosh sets a novel in the Sundarbans, the mangrove archipelago of the Ganga–Brahmaputra delta, called by locals bhatir desh, "the tide country" (p. 10). Two outsiders meet on the train to Canning (p. 7). Piya Roy, an American cetologist of Bengali descent who speaks no Bengali, has come to survey the rare Irrawaddy dolphin (Orcaella brevirostris). Kanai Dutt, a 42-year-old Delhi businessman who runs a translation bureau, is going to Lusibari because his aunt Nilima has found a notebook left for him by his uncle Nirmal, who died in 1979. After a corrupt forest guard's launch dumps her in the river (p. 37), Piya is rescued by Fokir, an illiterate crab-fisherman, who leads her to a dolphin pool at Garjontola. They share no language but work together almost perfectly.

Kanai reads the notebook. It tells of the 1978–79 settlement of Morichjhãpi by Dalit refugees from Bangladesh (who had escaped a government camp at Dandakaranya), and of Kusum, Fokir's mother, who was killed when the West Bengal government evicted them to protect a tiger reserve (pp. 94–99, 214–215). In Part Two all four sail on Horen's boat, the Megha. Villagers trap a man-eating tiger and burn it alive while Piya tries to stop them (pp. 236–241). Kanai and Piya argue about who pays the human cost of conservation (pp. 245–246). Fokir abandons Kanai on Garjontola, where he faces a tiger and is "judged" (pp. 265–269). A cyclone hits. Fokir shields Piya with his body, tied to a tree, and is killed (pp. 309–315); the notebook is lost. In the epilogue Piya returns to Lusibari to run a dolphin project with Nilima's trust, built on Fokir's knowledge, saved on her GPS and named after him (pp. 319–323). The novel's argument is that nature and culture cannot be separated here, that conservation imposed from above can be a form of violence against the poor, and that people with very different kinds of knowledge must learn to share the land.

Plot step by step

Each step gives what happens, the page, and the theme it carries. The bold line is what to remember.

  1. Two outsiders on the train to Canning (pp. 7–17)

    Kanai, a polyglot translator ("Language was both his livelihood and his addiction", p. 7), notices Piya, who knows no Bengali (p. 8). He pulls an old seat-mate aside by sheer class confidence (p. 9). The notebook's opening describes the tide country: Ganga's braid "comes undone", an archipelago where "the boundaries between land and water are always mutating" and where the terrain is hostile to humans (pp. 9–10). Piya tells Kanai the river dolphins have "kind of disappeared from view" because everyone only talks about "the tigers and the crocodiles" (p. 14).

    • Remember: both main viewpoints belong to outsiders. The novel will test how well outsiders can know this place.

  2. Nilima, Nirmal and the notebook (pp. 18–28, 57–68)

    At Canning Kanai meets his aunt Nilima, who runs the Badabon Trust and hospital on Lusibari. Nirmal, her husband, a former Marxist and Rilke-loving schoolmaster, died in 1979 "around the time of the Morichjhãpi incident" (p. 25). The notebook begins: "I am writing these words in a place… called Morichjhãpi" (p. 58). Nirmal and Nilima came to Lusibari in 1950 and found "destitution" in Hamilton's supposed utopia (pp. 64–67).

    • Remember: the novel is built in layers. Kanai's present-day story frames Nirmal's 1979 story, so history is always read through a document.

  3. Piya's fall and Fokir's rescue (pp. 29–42, 47–50, 55–63)

    The Forest Department forces Piya to take a guard and a launch (p. 30). The guard harasses fishermen and extorts them. When Piya falls into the river, Fokir dives in and saves her (p. 37 on). She shows him her dolphin card and he points straight at the Irrawaddy dolphin (p. 41). She hires him. His boat is "the nautical equivalent of a shanty" (p. 60). The guard's behaviour shows how the state's "conservation" looks to locals: bribes, bullying, and armed men.

    • Remember: local knowledge (Fokir) beats official authority (the guard) from the first meeting.

  4. Hamilton's dream (pp. 33–36, 43–46)

    Sir Daniel Hamilton, a Scottish businessman, bought ten thousand acres of the tide country in 1903 (p. 44) to build a cooperative society without caste or exploitation. "Look how much this mud is worth… A single acre of Bengal's mud yields fifteen maunds of rice" (p. 43). Settlers cleared the forest and "hundreds" were killed by tigers, crocodiles and snakes (p. 45). Nirmal calls it a "mirage" (p. 43).

    • Remember: the first human settlement here was itself an act of clearing the forest. This complicates any idea that the Sundarbans is untouched "wilderness".

  5. Kusum and the Bon Bibi story (pp. 73–89)

    Kanai remembers his punishment visit to Lusibari as a boy and the girl Kusum. At a performance of the Bon Bibi legend, she explains Bon Bibi's law: half the forest for the demon Dokkhin Rai, half for humans, "the wild and the sown… held in careful balance" (p. 84). Kusum saw a tiger kill her father (p. 88). She will not say the word bagh: "to say it is to call it" (p. 88). Horen hurries her away because Dilip, a trafficker, has come looking for her (p. 89).

    • Remember: local religion has its own ethic of limits: take only what you need from the forest. This is the islanders' version of "conservation".

  6. What happened at Morichjhãpi (pp. 94–99)

    Nilima explains: in 1978 thousands of refugees, "most of them… Dalits" (p. 95), broke out of the Dandakaranya resettlement camp in central India ("more like a concentration camp", p. 96) and cleared Morichjhãpi, an island inside a tiger reserve. The new Left Front government declared it a protected forest and evicted them. The final clash came in May 1979 (p. 96). Kusum "died in the massacre" (p. 98).

    • Remember: this is a real event. It is the novel's clearest case of conservation as displacement.

  7. Piya and Fokir at the dolphin pool (pp. 90–93, 100–103, 110–115, 121–124)

    Fokir leads Piya to a deep pool at Garjontola where Orcaella gather at low tide. Her "epiphany": these dolphins compress their yearly Mekong rhythm into the daily tide (p. 101). She realises the project could fill her life (p. 102). They share no language, yet she feels he understands her.

    • Remember: science and local knowledge cooperate here. Fokir already knew everything her instruments measure.

  8. Nirmal on Morichjhãpi (pp. 129–140, 144–156, 163–167, 181–192, 200–203, 207–215)

    In the notebook, Nirmal and Horen are blown ashore on Morichjhãpi and find Kusum, now a widow, and young Fokir (p. 130). Kusum tells her story in a rhythmic, almost sung voice (pp. 131–133). Nirmal is excited by the settlers' self-organised society (a feast, p. 153). Nilima refuses to let the Trust help (p. 173). Kusum takes Nirmal to Garjontola for Bon Bibi's puja and the dolphins appear as Bon Bibi's "messengers" (pp. 190–191). Then the siege: police cut off food and water, destroy tube wells, and settlers eat grass (p. 214). Kusum's speech follows (p. 215).

    • Remember: Kusum gives the novel's strongest critique of "global" conservation. Her crime was being "just human beings, trying to live as human beings always have" (p. 215).

  9. Moyna, Fokir and the Megha (pp. 104–109, 168–172, 193–199, 216–221)

    Moyna, Fokir's wife, trains as a nurse and wants Tutul educated; she calls Fokir "illiterate" and says he cannot understand her plans (p. 108). Kanai is attracted to her. Piya hires Horen's boat, the Megha, for a longer survey, with Kanai as translator and Fokir as guide.

    • Remember: Moyna represents modernity and upward mobility from within the village. She is neither the "authentic" local nor the outsider.

  10. The tiger is burnt alive (pp. 236–241)

    A tiger that "had killed two people" in the village (p. 238) is trapped in a cattle pen, blinded and speared. Villagers shout "Maar! Maar! Kill! Kill!" Piya pleads ("You can't take revenge on an animal", p. 239), snaps a spear, and is dragged away by Fokir, who sides with the villagers (p. 240). The pen is set on fire.

    • Remember: the novel's mirror image of Morichjhãpi. There the state killed humans for tigers; here humans kill a tiger. Neither side is simply right.

  11. The argument (pp. 242–247)

    Piya calls the killing "the horror" and says it makes Fokir "a part of the horror". Kanai replies that tigers kill people "every week", and "if there were killings on that scale anywhere else on earth it would be called a genocide… these people are too poor to matter" (p. 245). He blames "people like you… who made a push to protect the wildlife here, without regard for the human costs" and Indians of his class who "hide these costs… to curry favor with their Western patrons" (p. 246). Piya answers that if we decide no other species matters, "it'll be people next — just the kind of people you're thinking of, people who're poor and unnoticed" (p. 246). Kanai apologises.

    • Remember: learn this exchange. It is the novel's debate on environmentalism of the rich vs. the poor, and both sides are given a real argument.

  12. Kanai judged at Garjontola (pp. 258–269)

    Fokir takes Kanai onto Garjontola to see tiger marks and switches from respectful apni to familiar tui: "in stepping onto the island, the authority of their positions had been reversed" (p. 265). Kanai falls in the mud and curses Fokir, and out come "the master's suspicion of the menial; the pride of caste; the townsman's mistrust of the rustic" (p. 265). He suddenly sees himself through Fokir's eyes, as one of "the men who had destroyed Fokir's village… killed his mother": Fokir "wanted him to be judged" (p. 266). Abandoned, he meets a tiger and survives (pp. 267–269).

    • Remember: Kanai's words fail in the forest ("without words it could not be apprehended", p. 268). Language, his power, means nothing here.

  13. Dead calf and Horen's secret (pp. 280–295)

    Piya and Fokir find a dolphin calf killed by the propeller of a fast official boat, probably police, coast guard or Forest Department (p. 281). Fokir has seen three such carcasses. Horen tells Kanai that he too loved Kusum, and that on her last night they joined "like that of fresh water and salt" (p. 295). Fokir sings the story of Dukhey's redemption (p. 287).

    • Remember: the dolphins are endangered by the state's own boats, not the fishermen. Another reversal of the "locals destroy nature" assumption.

  14. The cyclone and Fokir's death (pp. 296–318)

    A cyclone arrives. Kanai loses Nirmal's notebook in the water (p. 312). At Lusibari, the tidal surge floods the island but people survive in the hospital's cyclone shelter, built at Nirmal's urging (p. 313). Nilima: "the dreamers have everyone to speak for them… those who try to build things — no one ever sees any poetry in that" (p. 313). On Garjontola, Piya and Fokir tie themselves to a tree with a sari. In the storm's eye they see a tiger swimming (p. 314). When the wind turns, Fokir's body becomes her shield: "the storm had given them what life could not; it had fused them together and made them one" (p. 315). He dies.

    • Remember: nature here is not a victim to be protected but an overwhelming force. And it is the poor local who dies.

  15. Home: the epilogue (pp. 319–323)

    Piya returns from Kolkata with funding from the internet (p. 320). She refuses work "that places the burden of conservation on those who can least afford it" and wants the project under the Badabon Trust "so the local fishermen would be involved", with Moyna managing it (p. 321). She and wants it named after Fokir because his routes, saved on her GPS, are "decades of work and volumes of knowledge" (p. 322). She plans to help Moyna and Tutul. Kanai will move to Kolkata and rewrite Nirmal's notebook (p. 323). Piya: "for me, home is where the Orcaella are" (p. 323).

    • Remember: the ending proposes a collaborative conservation that includes local people and their knowledge. Critics still ask whether the local man had to die for the outsider to belong.

Characters

Exams ask you to "consider at least two characters" and their "ways of understanding and inhabiting the Sundarbans". Each card gives that angle.

Piyali (Piya) Roy

US-raised cetologist of Bengali parents, speaks no Bengali. Sees the delta as a habitat to be studied and protected. Her tools: GPS, depth sounder, data sheets. Learns that conservation has a human cost, and ends by staying and working with locals.

Way of knowing: science · Way of living: outsider who chooses to belong

Kanai Dutt

42, Delhi, runs a translation business; urbane, vain, a womaniser. Sees the place through words and texts (the notebook). Translates between Piya and Fokir, and controls what each hears. Humbled on Garjontola, where words fail. Voice of the "human costs" argument.

Way of knowing: language · Way of living: metropolitan visitor

Fokir

Kusum's son, illiterate fisherman, Moyna's husband, Tutul's father. Knows the rivers, tides and dolphins with his body; sings the Bon Bibi story. Helps kill the tiger, yet respects the forest. Dies shielding Piya. Mostly seen from outside: he rarely speaks.

Way of knowing: lived, oral, bodily · Way of living: part of the ecosystem

Nirmal Bose

Kanai's uncle, retired Lusibari headmaster, ex-Marxist, loves Rilke and geology. Sees in Morichjhãpi the revolution he never had. His notebook is a layer of the novel. Died in 1979. His practical legacy is the cyclone shelter ("just social service — not revolution", p. 313).

Way of knowing: history, poetry, ideology

Nilima Bose

Nirmal's wife, founder of the Badabon Trust and hospital. Pragmatic reformer who works with the state, so she would not help Morichjhãpi. "The challenge of making a few little things a little better in one small place is enough" (p. 312).

Way of living: patient, institutional reform

Kusum

Fokir's mother. Lost her father to a tiger (p. 88), trafficked, widowed, joined the Morichjhãpi settlers. Speaks for the displaced poor: "our crime was that we were just human beings" (p. 215). Killed in the 1979 massacre.

Voice of: the refugee, environmental injustice

Moyna Mandol

Fokir's wife, trainee nurse at the Trust hospital, ambitious for Tutul's schooling. Calls Fokir "illiterate" (p. 108). Shows that locals also want modern lives; the "authentic native" is a myth.

Voice of: aspiration, modernity from within

Horen Naskor

Fisherman and boatman, owner of the Megha. Took Nirmal to Morichjhãpi; secretly loved Kusum (p. 295); Fokir's protector. Reads tides and storms by experience.

Link between: the 1979 story and the present

Tutul

Fokir and Moyna's son. The future generation; Piya promises to support him.

Sir Daniel Hamilton

Real Scottish businessman who bought ten thousand acres in 1903 (p. 44) for a cooperative utopia (pp. 43–46). Colonial "improvement" of the land.

Key terms

Tide country (bhatir desh)

Local name for the Sundarbans. "Bhati" is the ebb tide: "it is only in falling that the water gives birth to the forest" (p. 10). Land and water swap places twice a day.

Sundarbans

"The beautiful forest" (p. 10). World's largest mangrove forest, across India and Bangladesh. Tiger reserve, home to millions of very poor people.

Morichjhãpi (1978–79)

Island (name means "Pepper Island", p. 58) settled by Dalit refugees who fled Dandakaranya. Evicted by the West Bengal government in the name of forest and tiger protection; siege and massacre in May 1979 (pp. 95–98, 214–215). A real historical event.

Bon Bibi

Forest goddess worshipped by Hindus and Muslims alike. She defeated the demon Dokkhin Rai and divided the forest between wild and human halves (p. 84). Her "law of the forest": "the rich and greedy would be punished while the poor and righteous were rewarded" (p. 86).

Dokkhin Rai

Demon-king of the forest who takes the form of a tiger; craves human flesh (p. 84).

Dukhey's story

Greedy merchant Dhona offers the poor boy Dukhey to Dokkhin Rai in exchange for wealth; Bon Bibi saves him (pp. 84–85, 287). A myth about greed, limits and the poor.

Conservation vs. survival

The core conflict. Top-down protection of tigers/forests (state, foreign donors, "Project Tiger") vs. the right of poor people to fish, clear land and live. Clearest in Kusum's speech (p. 215) and the Piya–Kanai debate (pp. 245–246).

Orcaella brevirostris

Irrawaddy dolphin, Piya's research subject (p. 41). Bon Bibi's "messengers" in local belief (p. 190). Science and myth describe the same animal.

Mohona

A confluence of several rivers, "an oddly seductive word" (p. 10).

Badabon Trust

Nilima's NGO and hospital on Lusibari. Practical, local development.

Dandakaranya

Government resettlement camp in central India for East Bengali refugees (p. 95); harsh land, unfamiliar language, guarded.

Garjontola

Fictional island with the dolphin pool and Bon Bibi shrine (pp. 121, 190). Where Kanai is "judged" and Fokir dies.

Snell's window

The circle of light through which a dolphin sees the world above water (p. 47). Metaphor for partial, species-bound points of view.

Translation

Kanai's trade, and a key theme. Piya and Fokir communicate without a shared language; Kanai's translations are sometimes self-serving. Can anyone "translate" the tide country?

Rob Nixon, Slow Violence

midsem text · key for Q6-type

Nixon from memory: slow violence is "violence that occurs gradually and out of sight… dispersed across time and space", attritional, not spectacular, and it falls mostly on the poor. It is a representational challenge because media prefer sudden events.

  • Invisible deaths: Kanai says tiger killings "are never reported, never written about in the papers… these people are too poor to matter" (p. 245). Pure slow violence.
  • Morichjhãpi: spectacular at the end (massacre) but slow in the siege (hunger, cholera, eating grass, p. 214) and slower still in decades of camps and displacement since Partition. A historical event almost erased from public memory.
  • The land itself: erosion, salinity, islands "made and unmade" (p. 183), and the hunger of settlers. Gradual harm.
  • Dead dolphins from official motorboats (p. 281): unseen, cumulative species loss.
  • The cyclone is the "fast" counterpart: spectacular, yet its toll falls on the same poor people (Fokir).
  • Nixon's "environmentalism of the poor" fits Kusum, whose fight for land is also an ecological claim. from memory
Strength of applying Nixon: explains why Morichjhãpi and tiger deaths stay invisible. Limit: the novel also has very spectacular violence (burning tiger, massacre, cyclone), and it gives agency to nature, which Nixon's human-centred frame underplays.

Mahasweta Devi, Pterodactyl, Puran Sahay and Pirtha

midsem text · Q5-type

from memory Journalist Puran visits Pirtha, a drought-hit, starving tribal (Nagesia) village, where a pterodactyl has appeared; the tribals see it as an ancestral soul. It dies; the tribal knowledge stays untranslatable to outsiders. A critique of "development" that ignores Adivasis.

  • Both: marginalised communities (Dalit refugees / Adivasis) whose knowledge is dismissed by "dominant institutions" (state, scientists, media).
  • Both: an outsider (Puran / Kanai, Piya) enters, fails to fully understand, and is changed.
  • Both: a non-human figure (pterodactyl / tiger, dolphin) carries indigenous memory and myth (Bon Bibi).
  • Development and displacement: Pirtha's starvation and land loss ≈ Morichjhãpi's eviction and Dandakaranya camps.
  • Difference: Pirtha's enemy is "development"; in Ghosh the enemy is conservation. Both show the state's projects serving outside interests.
  • Vulnerability + inequality: in both, ecological disaster (drought / cyclones, tigers) hits hardest those already poorest.

Coleridge, The Rime of the Ancient Mariner

midsem text

from memory The Mariner kills the albatross, suffers, and is redeemed when he blesses the water-snakes "unaware".

  • Killing an animal: the albatross vs. the burnt tiger (pp. 236–241). Coleridge treats it as sin; Ghosh shows a village that has lost people to that tiger. Compare the moral frames.
  • Storms and the sea as a force beyond human control: the Mariner's ship vs. the cyclone (p. 309).
  • Transformation: Kanai on Garjontola, like the Mariner, is humbled and sees the world differently.

Gun Island (Ghosh, 2019)

sequel · endsem text
  • Same world: Kanai, Nilima, Piya and Moyna return. Tipu, the boy bitten by the cobra, is Tutul grown up, Moyna and Fokir's son. Piya's dolphins (Rani) die in Gun Island.
  • Hungry Tide is about staying (who can live here); Gun Island is about leaving (climate migration out of the delta).
  • Both use a Bengali legend (Bon Bibi / Manasa Devi) as a carrier of ecological knowledge.

The Great Derangement (Ghosh, 2016)

endsem text
  • In GD Ghosh says he first felt the Sundarbans' instability while writing The Hungry Tide: the landscape is "alive" and moves. See "transformation is the rule of life" (p. 183).
  • GD argues realist novels exclude the improbable. The Hungry Tide already lets in a cyclone, a tiger in a storm's eye (p. 314), and Bon Bibi's "messengers" (p. 190), but it still explains them mostly in realist terms.
  • Nature as an agent ("determination to destroy or expel them", p. 10) is GD's "non-human forces" idea in fiction.

Arundhati Roy, The Cost of Living

endsem text
  • Roy writes about people displaced by big dams (Narmada) with no rehabilitation. Morichjhãpi is the same pattern: the state displaces the poor (mostly Dalits and Adivasis) for a "national" goal.
  • Roy's "who pays the cost of development?" = Kanai's "who pays the human cost of conservation?" (p. 246).
  • Both critique the urban Indian elite who hide the costs (Kanai: "Indians of my class… curry favor with their Western patrons").

Orijit Sen, River of Stories

endsem text
  • Also about rivers, Adivasi myth and displacement (Narmada dam). Both mix myth with modern politics.
  • Both use an outsider (a journalist in Sen; Kanai/Piya in Ghosh) whose view is challenged by local knowledge.
  • Myth as resistance: Bon Bibi's balance of wild and sown vs. the Adivasi creation stories of the river.

Jamieson / Heise, Anthropocene: Love It or Leave It

endsem text
  • "Anthropocene" says humans now shape nature. The Sundarbans shows that "humans" are not one group: the cost falls on the poor, not on those who caused it.
  • There is no "pristine nature" to protect: Hamilton's settlements were cleared forest, and Morichjhãpi was the same. This supports a critique of "wilderness" thinking.
  • The cyclone shows nature is not just shaped by humans; it overwhelms them.

Strengths and limits

Strengths

  • Gives both sides of conservation vs. survival a real voice (Piya and Kanai, p. 246). No easy villain.
  • Recovers a forgotten real event (Morichjhãpi) and links caste to ecology.
  • Treats nature as an active force (tides, cyclone, tigers), not scenery.
  • Shows several kinds of knowledge (science, myth, lived skill) and how they can combine (Fokir's GPS map, p. 322).
  • Rich form: notebook, legend, songs, science, mixing genres the way the tide mixes fresh water and salt.

Limits / critiques

  • Fokir is silent: the subaltern is seen only through outsiders; he barely speaks. (Spivak's "Can the subaltern speak?" question.)
  • The local man dies so the outsiders (Piya, Kanai) can be transformed and belong. Some call this a romantic sacrifice.
  • The ending (internet funding, NRI visa, a project in his name) may be too neat and still led by an outsider.
  • Morichjhãpi is told second-hand through Nirmal's romantic eyes.
  • Tigers have no "voice" like humans do; the non-human is still mainly a problem for humans.

Likely 10-mark questions

Q1–Q3 follow your midsem's Q1, Q5 and Q6. Each outline is a full answer plan: intro, 3–4 body points with evidence, conclusion.

Q1How does The Hungry Tide complicate the relationship between human communities and the environment? Use at least two characters to show competing ways of understanding and inhabiting the Sundarbans.

Intro

The Sundarbans blurs land/water, human/non-human, nature/culture (p. 10). Ghosh uses characters as different "ways of seeing" the same place. Thesis: no single way is enough; the novel argues for combining them, and shows who pays when one dominates.

Body

  1. Piya (science/conservation): sees a habitat; data, GPS, dolphins. Opposes the tiger killing: "You can't take revenge on an animal" (p. 239). Argues all species matter: "it'll be people next" (p. 246).
  2. Fokir (lived knowledge): knows tides and dolphins by experience; the dolphins are Bon Bibi's "messengers" (p. 190). Yet he joins the tiger killing (p. 240): he lives with the danger. Inhabits rather than studies.
  3. Kanai (language/urban elite): sees through words; argues "human costs" (p. 246); in the forest, his words fail and he is "judged" (pp. 265–266).
  4. Kusum / Nirmal: Morichjhãpi as the human right to live "from the water and the soil" (p. 215) vs. the state's tiger reserve.
  5. Bon Bibi offers a local model: wild and sown "held in careful balance" (p. 84).

Conclusion

Epilogue: Piya's project built on Fokir's knowledge (p. 322) suggests collaboration; but his death shows the unequal price.

Q2Compare The Hungry Tide and Pterodactyl as critiques of development, displacement and environmental injustice. How do they link ecological vulnerability and social inequality?

Intro

Both depict marginal communities (Dalit refugees; Adivasis) whose lives depend on ecology and whose knowledge is dismissed by the state.

Body

  1. Displacement: Dandakaranya "concentration camp" (p. 96) and Morichjhãpi eviction ↔ Pirtha's land loss and starvation from memory.
  2. Development vs. conservation: Mahasweta targets development; Ghosh targets conservation. Both are state projects for outsiders ("paid for by people from all around the world", p. 215).
  3. Caste/tribe: "Most of them were Dalits" (p. 95); tribals in Pirtha. Inequality decides who is vulnerable.
  4. Vulnerability: tiger deaths "too poor to matter" (p. 245), cyclone kills Fokir ↔ drought and famine in Pirtha.
  5. Outsider witness: Kanai/Piya ↔ Puran. Both are changed, but can they speak for the community?
  6. Myth and memory: Bon Bibi ↔ the pterodactyl as ancestor.

Conclusion

Both show ecological and social injustice are the same problem: the poor bear the costs of others' plans.

Q3Using Rob Nixon's "slow violence", analyse The Hungry Tide. Strengths and limits of applying the framework?

Intro

Define slow violence: gradual, dispersed, out of sight, hitting the poor; hard to represent. Literature can make it visible.

Body

  1. Unreported tiger deaths (p. 245): "never reported… too poor to matter".
  2. Morichjhãpi siege: starvation, cholera, eating grass (p. 214), then erased from memory (Kanai "doesn't recall it", p. 25).
  3. Long displacement: Partition → Dandakaranya → Morichjhãpi: decades of attrition.
  4. Ecological attrition: dolphins killed by official boats (p. 281); unstable land (p. 183).
  5. How the novel represents it: Nirmal's notebook as testimony; Kusum's voice.

Strengths / limits

Strength: explains invisibility and class. Limits: much violence here is spectacular (massacre, burning tiger, cyclone); Nixon centres humans while Ghosh gives nature agency; slow violence here is caused by conservation, not only by pollution/extraction.

Q4"Conservation vs. survival." Discuss with reference to Morichjhãpi and the tiger killing.
  1. Morichjhãpi: humans evicted for tigers. Kusum: "Who are these people… who love animals so much…" (p. 215).
  2. Tiger killing: humans kill a tiger that killed two villagers (pp. 238–240). Mirror image.
  3. Debate: Kanai's "human costs" vs. Piya's "it'll be people next" (pp. 245–246).
  4. Bon Bibi's balance (p. 84) as alternative. Epilogue: Piya rejects conservation that burdens "those who can least afford it" and involves local fishermen (p. 321).
  5. Conclude: the novel refuses to choose; it asks who decides and who pays.
Q5Discuss the role of language and translation in the novel.
  1. Kanai: "Language was both his livelihood and his addiction" (p. 7). He controls Piya–Fokir communication.
  2. Piya and Fokir connect without words; Fokir's map is "volumes of knowledge" (p. 322).
  3. Kusum: "to say it is to call it" (p. 88). Words have power in the forest.
  4. Kanai in the mud: "without words it could not be apprehended" (p. 268). Language fails.
  5. Nirmal's notebook: history survives in writing and is then lost; Kanai must rewrite it (p. 323).
Q6How do myth and legend (Bon Bibi) function as environmental knowledge?
  1. Bon Bibi divides wild and sown (p. 84): a rule of limits.
  2. "Law of the forest": greed punished (p. 86); Dukhey's story vs. greedy Dhona.
  3. Worshipped by Hindus and Muslims: shared, syncretic culture of the tide country.
  4. Dolphins as "messengers" (p. 190) = the same pool Piya studies. Myth and science meet.
  5. Link to Gun Island (Manasa Devi legend) and River of Stories; GD's call to take stories seriously.
Q7Compare Nirmal and Nilima as two models of social change in the Sundarbans.
  1. Nirmal: Marxist dreamer, poetry and revolution; drawn to Morichjhãpi (p. 96).
  2. Nilima: practical reformer; the Trust and hospital; "a few little things a little better" (p. 312).
  3. Nilima refuses to help Morichjhãpi (p. 173): pragmatism has a moral cost.
  4. Irony: Nirmal's lasting gift is the cyclone shelter, "social service — not revolution" (p. 313).
  5. "The dreamers have everyone to speak for them" (p. 313). The novel values both.
Q8How is the non-human (tide, tiger, dolphin, cyclone) represented as an agent in the novel?
  1. Land "determined to destroy or expel" humans (p. 10); "transformation is the rule of life" (p. 183).
  2. Tiger: feared, worshipped, killed; never named (p. 88); seen in the storm's eye (p. 314).
  3. Dolphins: follow the tides (p. 101); studied, sacred, killed by boats (p. 281).
  4. Cyclone: kills Fokir, floods Lusibari (pp. 313–315).
  5. Link to GD (non-human agency) and the Anthropocene debate.

Exam tips and traps

  • Write "Morichjhãpi (1978–79)" with the date and the word Dalit refugees. It shows you know it is history.
  • Don't say Fokir "protected the tiger". He helped kill it (p. 240). That is the point: locals are not romantic eco-saints.
  • Don't make Piya the villain. Her argument ("it'll be people next", p. 246) is strong. Give both sides, then judge.
  • Bon Bibi is worshipped by Hindus and Muslims. Mention it for "culture" questions.
  • The title: the tide is "hungry" because it eats land and people. The forest itself is hostile (p. 10).
  • Name the two parts: The Ebb (Bhata) and The Flood (Jowar).
  • Say "PDF p." only if asked; otherwise quote the words and the scene.
  • Always link to at least one other course text. Nixon and Pterodactyl are the safest; Gun Island and GD show you know Ghosh's career.
